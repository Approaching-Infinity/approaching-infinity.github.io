# A clearer picture of the month ahead

**Review Draft · Financial literacy · Updated October 30, 2026 · Free resource**

A bill calendar puts two everyday questions on one page: what is coming due, and when? This 15-minute exercise is a starting point for seeing the month ahead. You can try it privately with paper, a calendar you already use, or the accompanying blank worksheet.

Approaching Infinity explores finance, technology and innovation. Here, the useful tool can be as simple as a pencil. The goal is to turn a broad subject into one small task you can actually try.

**Learning goal:** By the end, you should be able to tell a bill’s due date from your planned payment date, record three bills, and identify one date or amount that needs checking.

## Start with a small view

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s [Your Money, Your Goals toolkit](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/educator-tool/your-money-your-goals/toolkit/) includes free tools for everyday money questions. Its [bill-calendar tool](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_your-money-your-goals_bill_calendar_tool_2018-11_ADA.pdf) organizes bills and income by date. This article offers an original, smaller starter exercise; the linked CFPB PDF provides its own instructions and worksheet.

You do not need to organize an entire financial life in one sitting. For this exercise, choose one month and three bills. Use statements or the biller’s official account information. If something is missing, write “check” rather than silently treating a guess as fact.

## Try this in 15 minutes

**Minutes 0–3: choose the month.** Open the [blank bill calendar worksheet](/resources/bill-calendar-worksheet/). Label the month and year. Choose three bills you want to record. Keep account numbers, passwords and other identifying details off the page. Use short labels such as “phone” or “electric.”

**Minutes 3–7: record what you know.** For each bill, write its due date and amount. If the amount varies, label it “estimate” until the statement is available. Include an automatic payment if one of your chosen bills uses it. An automatic payment still belongs in your picture of the month.

**Minutes 7–11: separate two dates.** The due date is the biller’s deadline. The planned payment date is when you intend to send payment, or when an automatic withdrawal is scheduled. Check the biller’s and payment service’s timing, including weekends or holidays. A calendar entry does not send money or change a deadline. Put the planned date on the calendar; keep the due date beside it in the bill list.

**Minutes 11–15: notice one question.** If useful, add expected income dates, clearly marking anything uncertain. Look for dates when several payments cluster. Choose one follow-up: verify an amount, check a scheduled withdrawal, or find the payment service’s processing information. Write when you will do it. Stop there if that is enough for today.

## One fictional example

Imagine Alex is recording a few bills for November 2026. Every name, amount and date here is invented. These are illustrative entries, not suggested spending levels or payment lead times.

| Bill | Due date | Planned payment date | Amount | Note |
|---|---|---|---:|---|
| Phone | November 12 | November 9 | $50 | Manual payment; timing assumed checked |
| Electricity | November 20 | November 17 | $85 | Estimate; statement still needed |
| Internet | November 25 | November 23 | $60 | Automatic withdrawal assumed confirmed |

These three entries total **$195**, including one estimate. Alex’s next step is to verify the electricity statement before November 17. That is a completed starter exercise, even though the page is still incomplete. The total is only the listed bills; it says nothing about whether Alex can afford the month.

## Know what the page leaves out

A bill calendar does not establish money available to spend. It leaves out starting balances, pending transactions and everyday costs unless you deliberately add them. For a broader next exercise, the CFPB’s [cash-flow budget tool](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_your-money-your-goals_cash_flow_budget_tool_2018-11_ADA.pdf) includes starting balances, income and expenses across weeks.

If the picture shows bills you cannot pay, that calls for more than rearranging a calendar. The CFPB’s [prioritizing-bills tool](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_your-money-your-goals_prioritizing-bills_tool.pdf) explains how to consider consequences and seek specialized help. This exercise does not decide payment priorities for you.

## Keep the next step small

At your next weekly check, revisit the item you marked “check” and update the page. You can add more bills when ready. Keep the worksheet private; there is no need to send it to Approaching Infinity.

**Optional anonymous feedback question:** After trying the exercise, was the difference between a due date and a planned payment date clear? Choose “yes,” “partly,” or “no.” No financial details are needed. A feedback collection route is not yet open in this review draft.
